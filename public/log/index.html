What it is actually made of. Strand glass-helix

A double helix of glass pipe with 12,000 particles running through it.

001-glass-catalogue0 authored geometry5 revisions2 failures

What it is actually made of. The helix is a formula, which is the important part: the same three lines drive the tube sweep on the CPU and every particle position in the vertex shader, so they cannot drift out of sync and nothing is updated per frame. Both strands are merged into a single geometry so there is one transmission pass instead of two. 12,000 particles ride through the pipe like water, over a dimmer inner tube of additive colour that gives the flow a body.

Dust smoke-veil

A sheath of very small motes orbiting the form's own long axis — coherent when driven, turbulent when let go, and occasionally catching the light.

002-scroll-helix0 authored geometry7 revisions2 failuresderived from scroll-helix

What it is actually made of. Stateless, on the same principle as the flow inside the tube: a particle's position is a pure function of its seed and four accumulators, so the whole veil costs four uniform writes a frame. Orbiting around the long axis rather than drifting past is what makes it cross in front of the glass and then behind it — the veil has to cross the form or it just sits beside it. About 30% of the particles orbit the opposite way so the cloud churns through itself instead of turning like a wheel. Scroll velocity feeds two separate accumulators: one adds swirl, the other drags the whole population along the axis, wrapped so it can never be emptied out.

The recipe

knobvalue
shellinner and depth both expressed as multiples of the glass's OUTER surface (radius + tube), never the centreline
radial biasshell fraction ** 1.6 — densest against the glass, thinning outward
orbitphase += delta × (orbit + |scrollVelocity| × orbitSurge); never zero
axial dragdrift += delta × scrollVelocity × follow, signed, per-particle drag 0.55–1.45 so it shears instead of sliding as a block
two statesa calm term eased toward 1 - min(1, |velocity| × 2.5); every turbulence term is multiplied by it
turbulencethree out-of-phase sine terms, not curl noise — nobody can resolve a single path at this size, only the aggregate
billowdisplaces the shell FRACTION, reflected at both ends, so radius cannot leave the sheath whatever the noise does
spritebroad gaussian, exp(-d²×12) minus the value at the rim so the discard leaves no visible edge
wrapfract() on the axial coordinate, with the outer eighth at each end faded out to hide the seam
lightinghalf-lambert against the shared light direction, ambient floor so the shadow side scatters rather than going dead black
sparklefacet term (half-vector, tightness 18) x per-particle flicker (sin^26) x a 14% reflective subset; a glinting mote also grows ~90%

addedasked: A-14lesson: L-21lesson: L-222026-07-28

Motes are lit by the shared light direction, and a subset of them sparkle. 14% are reflective at all; a half-vector facet term decides where a glint is possible and a per-particle flicker decides when, so roughly 98 of 40,000 are glinting at any instant, all on the lit side.

Dust that does not catch the light is just noise on the frame. Splitting the sparkle into a 'where' and a 'when' is what stops it reading as the whole field blinking — verified numerically rather than by eye, because a still frame catches an arbitrary instant of an intermittent effect.

Flakes hex-plates

Rare, much larger hexagonal plates riding the same orbit as the dust, tumbling like falling snow and flashing iridescent when a face catches the light.

002-scroll-helix0 authored geometry3 revisionsderived from smoke-veil

What it is actually made of. The one thing in the sheath with a face. Everything else here is a point sprite, which is always square-on to the camera and can therefore only brighten; a plate can turn, and turning is what lets it catch the light, sweep colour and vanish edge-on. It rides the identical orbit as the dust — literally the same GLSL and the same update function, pulled out into a shared module, because 'behaves the same way' is a requirement that two copies would quietly break. The tumble runs on its own clock rather than the orbit's: snow keeps turning at the same lazy rate whether or not it is being blown along.

The recipe

knobvalue
orbitshared with the dust, same module, same parameters
tumbletwo angles at unrelated per-plate rates plus a flutter term; no shared period, so no visible loop
measured tumble~38s per full turn; fastest plate 12x the slowest
basisreference vector swaps when the normal nears vertical, or the cross product collapses once per tumble
lightinglit by its own face, not by where it sits on the shell — a plate underneath with its face turned up really is catching the key
edge-onalpha scales with how square-on the plate is to the viewer, so it thins to nothing sideways
shineabs(dot(normal, half)) ^ 16 — abs because a flake is thin enough that either face can take the light
iridescencethree offset cosines driven by the viewing angle; carried at a base level at all times and pushed to full by a flash, so the colour does not depend on catching the light
measured flash~11 of 220 plates flashing at any instant, each one lit ~2% of the time

Panels card-panels

Large frosted glass slabs standing forward of the form, carrying the navigation and overlapping the helix without hiding it.

002-scroll-helix0 authored geometry2 revisions1 failures

What it is actually made of. Half WebGL and half DOM, on purpose. The panel is an extruded rounded rectangle with a bevel — the bevel is most of why it reads as a slab rather than a decal, since it gives the rim a surface at an angle to the face that can pick up the key and bend what is behind it. The label stays a real DOM anchor sitting on top, because that was this example's actual finding and it is not worth trading for a material. All five panels are merged into ONE geometry: the transmission material re-renders the whole scene into a buffer every frame, so five separate panels would have meant five extra full scene renders per frame on top of the one the tube already costs.

Screens card-screens

A different loop behind every panel, kept distant by the glass in front of it, throwing raked light forward through it.

002-scroll-helix0 authored geometry3 revisions1 failuresderived from card-panels

What it is actually made of. Two pieces that only make sense together. The screen is never drawn sharp and is never blurred either — the panel in front is frosted and its transmission pass diffuses whatever is behind it, so the material does the work and the setback distance is the whole distance control. The beam is a stack of soft slices lying in the plane the camera always faces, marching along a raked axis and coloured, six times a second, by the average colour of the footage at that moment. One video element feeds all five panels, each taking a different crop of the same frame.

The recipe

knobvalue
diffusionnone applied — the panel's own transmission pass does it; setback is the distance knob
clearancethe screen must stay outside 1.45 from the axis or it pokes through the form
distancedesaturation rather than dimming — colour goes before light does
beam22 slices in the XY plane, which in this scene is camera-facing because the camera travels but never turns
rakeblended from the scene's own light direction — light leaves a window travelling away from what lit it, and an unraked beam is seen down its own axis and has no length
striationtwo incommensurate frequencies across the beam; without it an unbroken rectangle emits a wash
coloursampled from a 1x1 canvas draw of the video, 6 times a second — the browser's own downscale is the averaging
fadebeams and screens fade with camera distance the same way the labels do, or every panel sprays light at once
decodersone per card, playing only inside a radius wider than the fade
placeholderpublic/media/panel-loop.mp4 — 39KB, generated, seamless palindrome loop; swap the src for real footage
sourcesfive 1.3-4.2s loops cut from the Blender Foundation's open movies (CC BY 3.0), 284KB total; credits in public/media/ATTRIBUTION.md
loopingseamless by crossfading the tail onto the head, not by palindrome — a palindrome reads as rewind on character animation
transparencynormal blending under full opacity, plus a luma key that holds back the dark end; additive is available and is not the default

How assets get built here

Six rules, every one of them paid for. The ones with a source attached were learned by getting it wrong first.

R-01 — Free assets are clay, not the final look.

A downloaded mesh with its default texture reads as a free mesh. The expensive feeling comes from lighting, grade, material, motion and post — the layers we control. Skip that and you get asset soup.

R-02 — Treatment belongs to the asset, not the page.from glass-eye

Learned the hard way on Ocellus. A four-panel rig that flatters a knot reflects off a sphere as grey rectangles. Every catalogue entry now carries its own light AND post recipe, and can opt out of the base rig entirely.

R-03 — Every aesthetic knob goes on a slider.

Taste is superb at picking and tuning and terrible at describing. Wiring the knobs to Leva means the director's eye drives the pixels directly instead of the subtlety having to survive translation into adjectives. Variant grids for the same reason — selection is effortless where specification is agony.

R-04 — Assets are deterministic.from glass-eye

Anything placed by a random draw gets a seeded PRNG. An asset that rolls a different lottery on every refresh cannot be art-directed — you would be tuning a thing that no longer exists.

R-05 — Folders stay liftable.

No example imports from another. helixCurve.js is duplicated on purpose rather than shared, so any folder can be copied out whole. Local assets only — no CDN fonts, no remote HDRI.

R-06 — Instrument rather than guess on performance.from L-13

The browser these are built through renders on the CPU, so no framerate number from the build process would mean anything. So the page measures itself on whatever GPU it lands on, adapts its own pixel ratio, and exposes every expensive knob. The stats panel is the only real evidence.

R-07 — Check shader geometry numerically, not just by eye.from L-18

A still frame cannot show orbit, containment or wrap, and those are exactly the properties that go quietly wrong. Re-run the shader maths on the CPU and assert the population-level claims. It is cheap, it runs without a GPU, and here it caught particles rendering inside the glass after the picture had already been signed off four times.

R-08 — One light direction, exported from one place.from L-19

Every part of a scene that draws a pixel has to agree about where the light is — the environment, the lamps, any baked vertex shading, and every particle shader. Four separate answers is how a scene ends up looking lit from nowhere. Elevation and azimuth on sliders, one vector derived from them, consumed everywhere.

R-09 — Shared behaviour lives in shared code.from L-24

When two things are specified to move the same way, put the motion in one module both consume. Copies satisfy the requirement once and then diverge silently under tuning — nothing errors, they just stop agreeing, and the drift is invisible until someone notices the scene looks wrong.

R-10 — Verify a backup by restoring it.from L-30

Writing the file proves the write succeeded and nothing else. Clone the bundle, extract the archive, compare against the original, and record how to do it next to the files. A restore point discovered to be bad at the moment it is needed is worse than none, because it was counted on.

R-11 — Prove a check can fail before trusting it to pass.from L-32

A test that has only ever returned the answer you wanted might be measuring nothing. Run it against a case known to be positive first. Here a wrong element id reported a control panel absent in both the build that hid it and the build that showed it.

R-12 — A living page names its own project in its heading.from L-33

One page per project, and it has to say which — the h1 carries the project, the document type sits above it as a kicker. A page led by its document type cannot be told apart from every other page built in the same format. Checked rather than remembered: the build fails when the project field is missing, when it matches a blocklist of generic document nouns, or when the rendered heading doesn't contain it.

R-13 — Let the material do the effect.from L-34

Before reaching for a filter, check whether something already in the scene produces it physically. Content placed behind glass is diffused by the glass, and that diffusion cannot drift out of step with the material the way a hand-matched blur would.

R-14 — Decode only what is near.from L-39

One video per card means one decoder per card, and five running at once is how a page like this stutters on a laptop. Play inside a radius wider than the fade, so a panel is already running by the time it can be seen, and pause everything else.

R-15 — Stop adding before it is obvious you should have.from L-41

Each effect here was justified on its own and the page still filled up. Nothing in the build tells you when to stop, because every individual addition renders correctly and looks defensible in isolation. Density is a decision made about the whole page, from outside it.

R-16 — Fit both axes, at the depth the content actually sits.from L-42

Solving a camera distance for one dimension leaves the other to the aspect ratio, and the failure only appears at ratios away from the one being worked at. Ask each piece of content how far back the camera must be for it, at its own depth, and take the largest answer.

Open

What is unresolved, unbuilt or unmeasured. Nothing here is being quietly hoped away.

Q-01 — The rotated vertical variant for portrait is still not built.from L-11

The one thing the scroll test genuinely failed. The mitigation in place is acceptable, not good, and everyone involved knows the difference.

Q-02 — Persistent canvas — the only decision here with a deadline.

If the WebGL context unmounts between pages you get a white flash and a shader recompile, and the illusion breaks completely. Hiding that seam means one application with a context that never dies and DOM content swapping around it. It cannot be retrofitted, so it has to be decided before anything real is scaffolded. It also settles a second question: whether the linked pages are further WebGL worlds or conventional documents.

Q-03 — The vehicle is not formally locked.

Both studies are R3F and it works, so it is the de-facto answer — but vanilla Three (dropping the React overhead for a pure performance showcase) and WebGPU/TSL (newer, rougher, genuinely ahead) were never formally ruled out.

Q-04 — Nobody has read a framerate off a real GPU yet.

The stats panel exists in both studies for exactly this. Until someone opens it on real hardware, every performance claim in this log is arithmetic rather than evidence. See L-13.

Q-05 — Composition is untested — everything here is one object, centred, on black.

The friendliest possible case, and the premise has only been tested inside it. Multi-element scenes with foreground, midground and background are where procedural work usually starts looking like a shader demo rather than a place.

Q-06 — In-scene typography is untested.

Text living inside the 3D scene rather than in the DOM layer over it. Different problem from the card anchors, which are solved.

Q-07 — Whether an articulated form following a spline needs modeling at all.

Repeated segments instanced along a curve is a procedural strength, not a weakness. It was conceded as needing real geometry and never actually tested — a cheap study, still unbuilt.

Q-08 — The house-style risk, which is art direction rather than technique.

Procedural work has a look. A great deal of it is technically extraordinary and completely anonymous — it reads as a shader demo. The gap between that and something that reads as a place built with intent is composition and restraint, and no amount of craft in this log closes it.

Q-10 — What 40k additional soft additive points cost.from L-13

They are cheap per vertex and the maths is trivial, but they are large soft sprites and the overdraw is real. Same problem as everything else here: it cannot be measured from the build environment, so the count sits on a slider and the stats panel is the only evidence.

Q-11 — Whether the key is too hot at the default.

The specular streak reads clearly as light from above, which was the ask, but it blows close to white on the upper faces and the form underneath it is a saturated magenta. That may be exactly right or it may look pasted on — it is a judgement about the picture, and it has only been seen through a CPU-rendered frame at 640x400.

Q-12 — Whether the flakes want to be scarcer and larger still.

The brief said rare and much bigger, and the defaults are one reading of that — 220 plates at a size where a couple are catching light in frame at any moment. Twice the size and half the number is a different picture and might be the better one. Only an eye on real hardware can pick.

Q-13 — The panels do not fade with distance but their labels do.

The DOM anchors fade with camera distance so they arrive and leave rather than popping. The glass does not — it is scenery and sits in the world permanently. At the frame edges that can mean a visible panel with no text on it. It may read as perfectly natural or as an oversight, and only watching it move will tell.

Q-14 — What the beams cost, and whether they survive real footage.

Twenty-two additive slices per panel is a lot of overdraw for something this subtle, and it has never been measured on a GPU. The placeholder is also a slow abstract gradient, which is the kindest possible input — real footage cuts, and a beam colour sampled six times a second across a hard cut may strobe.

Q-15 — The page transition, which stops being optional the moment there is a second page.

Clicking a card does a normal page load — the one seam in this study still fully visible, and the only one that cannot be hidden by tuning something. Carrying the world across a navigation needs a WebGL context that outlives the page, with the DOM swapping around it. That is an architecture decision, and it is now the first one, because linked pages are the next piece of work.

Q-16 — Whether a landscape phone wants this composition at all.

It fits now, which is not the same as being designed for. A 2.2:1 frame 390 pixels tall gets the form and a panel and very little air, and the honest answer may be a different arrangement rather than a further-back camera — the same argument already made for portrait, from the other end.
